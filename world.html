03 / Reconstructing Toronto · c.1680 and c.1700 · in progress

The ground before the town.

Around 1700 there was no town here. There was a bay behind a long sandbar, a marsh at the mouth of the Don, forest to the water’s edge, and a trail up the Humber, the Carrying Place, that people had walked for centuries to reach the upper lakes. On it stood Teiaiagon, a Seneca village of bark longhouses with fields round it, left in 1687 or by 1701. The switch on the picture shows the place at two moments: about 1680, with the village standing, and about 1700, after. It is built on real ground and the oldest shoreline the maps can give, and every view says what it shows and what in it is real.

Six snapshots from the world

What's real here, and what isn't yet

  • RealThe shape of the ground: the Humber and Don valleys, the ravines and the old lake bluff. It comes from today’s elevation model (), because the land’s broad shape has not changed. Heights are stretched 2.5 times so the relief can be seen.
  • RealThe water’s edge, the marsh and the creeks, with one large caveat: nothing was mapped in 1700. They are traced from the earliest maps that cover each place, (). Land made since then has been taken back out, and the sand peninsula put back where those maps draw it.
  • RealWhere Teiaiagon stood. It was a village of the Seneca, one of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) nations, on the east bank of the Humber at what is now Baby Point, on the trail. Estimates of its size run from 500 to 800 people to about 2,000, and accounts differ on when it was left, in 1687 or by 1701 (Wikipedia, Teiaiagon; Wikipedia, History of Toronto). Its place is certain.
  • MixedThe village as built here, about 1680. From the record of Teiaiagon itself: 20 to 30 buildings and 500 to 800 people by one estimate, traces of a palisade found in the 1800s (the notes are lost), and fields all round. From what is known of Haudenosaunee longhouses, and from the Seneca longhouse rebuilt at Ganondagan in New York: houses about 24 m long and 5.5 m wide and high, some longer, with straight bark walls, a pitched roof, a door at each end and smoke holes along the ridge (Wikipedia, Longhouses; photograph of the Ganondagan longhouse). The palisade posts are 4 m, as a visitor measured at a Seneca town in 1669, though another in 1677 wrote that Seneca towns had none (Wikipedia, Ganondagan). Where each house stood is arranged by hand: no plan or picture of the village survives. Its burial ground lay in the middle; that ground is left open and nothing is drawn on it. Corn, beans and squash were grown in hills (Wikipedia, Three Sisters); only the corn is drawn, and how thickly is a guess.
  • MixedThe people’s dress. It follows the four portraits John Verelst painted in 1710 of three Mohawk leaders and one Mohican (Library and Archives Canada, on Wikimedia Commons): white linen shirts to the thigh, a patterned sash, a wide scarlet cloth cloak, moccasins with a wrapped ankle, dark hair cut short. Written accounts add leggings, a short kilt, a skirt and cape for women, men’s hair shaved to a tuft, a married woman’s single braid, and red and blue trade cloth replacing hide (Wikipedia, Iroquois). Limits: the portraits are of Mohawk and Mohican men, not Seneca, painted in London in clothes for meeting the Queen; no picture of a woman of these years was looked at; tattoos and ornaments are left out; and the figures are simple shapes.
  • DrawnWhat the people are doing: walking the trail, standing at the doors, bending to the corn with a digging stick, carrying a basket on the back. These are everyday acts chosen for the picture, not recorded moments. The figures are shapes made in code, with joints so they can stand, bend and walk; they are not portraits of anyone.
  • MixedThree places where a rule, not a measurement, shapes the land. The sandbar comes out of the data in pieces, because it is narrower than the 50 m spacing; it is recorded as joined to the shore until 1858, so the pieces are joined across their gaps. Ground the old maps draw as water but which stands above the lake today, close to the lake, is land made since and is put back under water. And a few specks of land alone in open water, where two map sheets disagree, are taken out.
  • MixedThe Carrying Place. The trail began at the lake and followed the Humber’s east bank north (Wikipedia, Toronto Carrying-Place Trail). Its exact line is not known to this project, so the line here simply keeps to that bank, and it is drawn about four metres wide so that it can be seen.
  • Stand-inThe fine detail of the ground, the bark and the ferns. The sand, the forest floor and the bark are photographs, and the fern is a scanned plant, all from Poly Haven (public domain). They were not photographed here and are not evidence of what grew at this shore; they are there so the ground does not look painted flat. On a phone the ferns are drawn instead.
  • Not realThe trees. Where each stands, what kind it is and how thick the forest grows are an impression, of them. The one exception is the scattered oaks at High Park, where black oak savannah still survives (Wikipedia, High Park).
  • Not realWhere each person and canoe is. They are placed by hand, and like the trees and houses they are drawn 1.5 times life size.
  • Not yetThe Anishinaabe, whom the French called Mississaugas, who moved into this country around 1700 (Wikipedia, Mississaugas). Nothing read for this project says where they camped here, so no camp is modelled in the c.1700 view. And the whole village needs checking with people who know: this reconstruction has not been seen by anyone from the Seneca Nation or the Mississaugas of the Credit.
  • Not yetThe lake bed, animals, seasons, the Rouge and its trail, and the shoreline east and west of the old maps, where today’s shore stands in.

The shoreline, marsh and creek data are adapted from the University of Toronto’s Historical Hydrography of Toronto, CC BY-SA 4.0.
