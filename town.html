One camera, a hundred and fifty years. In 1700 the forest ran down to the bay. In 1751 the French built a small fort and in 1759 burned it. In 1793 a garrison and ten blocks of town were laid out; by 1850 about thirty thousand people lived in a city with wharves reaching into the bay. Play the timelapse, or drag the years yourself. The dates and the counts of people are from sources; the houses and streets are drawn to match them, and the page says which is which.

What's real here, and what is drawn

  • RealThe ground, the shore, the sandbar, the marsh and the creeks. They are the same as in the c.1700 world, from today’s elevation and the earliest maps. The shore is held as it was then for the whole timelapse; the filling of the waterfront came later and is not shown.
  • RealThe dates. The French storehouse on the Humber (1720), the trading post of 1750 and Fort Rouillé (1751, burned by its own garrison in 1759); the Toronto Purchase (1787, revised 1805); the founding of York on 27 August 1793; parliament meeting in June 1797 and the extension west to Peter Street; the market (1803); the first church and first brick house (1807); the Battle of York (27 April 1813); incorporation as Toronto (6 March 1834); gas lamps (1841); the Cathedral Fire (7 April 1849). From Wikipedia, York, Upper Canada, Wikipedia, History of Toronto and The Friends of Fort York.
  • RealThe lighthouse on Gibraltar Point: begun in 1808, lit in 1809, 52 feet tall and raised to 82 in 1832, a six-sided stone tower (Wikipedia). It is placed at its recorded position.
  • MixedThe number of people. The counts used are 3 in 1793, about 240 in 1799, about 500 in 1808, 720 in 1813, 1,600 in 1825, 2,860 in 1830, 5,500 in 1832, 9,250 in 1834 and about 30,000 in 1851, from the two Wikipedia pages above. Those pages also give 600 for 1796 and 1,460 for 1812, which do not fit the others and are left out. Between counts the model fills in smoothly.
  • MixedWhere the named places stand. Fort York and Fort Rouillé are at their sites; the parliament is on the east side of the first town plot, the market and the church near King and Church streets. Their shapes are simple stand-ins: no plan or picture of any of them was used.
  • MixedEight buildings that still stand: The Grange (1817), the Bank of Upper Canada (1827), Osgoode Hall (1832), Little Trinity (1843), the city hall on Front Street (1844), the Church of the Holy Trinity (1847), St Michael’s Cathedral (1848) and St Lawrence Hall (1850). Their years and addresses are from Wikipedia’s list of the oldest buildings in Toronto. Each is placed at its address today by eye, good to about a block. Their shapes only say what kind of building each is.
  • DrawnEvery ordinary house. There are of them by 1850, and each goes up as the population grows. A small town still has to be seen, so one drawn house stands for about people in 1800 and about in 1850. Early ones are low and of wood; later ones are more often brick, and taller along the main streets. Where each stands is not from any map.
  • DrawnThe streets, as a regular grid turned the way Front Street runs. The first town was ten blocks and did follow a grid, but the real streets were not this even. Yonge Street is drawn running north from the 1790s.
  • DrawnHow fast the forest was cut. The first two steps follow the record (ten blocks in 1793, west to Peter Street in 1797); after that the clearing is drawn to keep ahead of the houses. trees stand in 1700 on ground that is bare by 1850.
  • DrawnThe wharves and the ships: how many there were in each year, and where. Buildings, like everything in this world, are drawn 1.5 times life size so they can be seen from the air.
  • Not yetThe Mississaugas of the Credit, on whose land the town was built, are named but not shown: no source read for this project places their camps. Nothing here has been reviewed by them. Also not yet: the 1837 rebellion, the railways of the 1850s, street names, and people at street level.

The shoreline, marsh and creek data are adapted from the University of Toronto’s Historical Hydrography of Toronto, CC BY-SA 4.0.
