03 / Reconstructing Toronto · 1800 to 2025 · a drawn film

A city taking shape.

One place, seen from the air for 225 years. Press Play and watch the forest become a town, and the town become the city you know.

1800The Town of York
18002025

The camera never moves: it hangs over the lake, looking across the islands to the town. Every picture is drawn from a 3D model of the real ground and turned into blue ink on old paper, the way an architect sketches a plan. The film takes 72 seconds to go from 1800 to 2025, about three years every second, and then rises to look straight down on the city today. Drag the year to go where you like. There is no sound.

Read this before you trust it. The shore, the ground, today’s buildings and the dated landmarks come from records. The year each ordinary building appears does not: nobody has a list of when every building in Toronto went up, so the film works it out from how far a building stands from the first town and how tall it is. The notes at the bottom say exactly which is which.

The timeline, period by period

Each period starts with what the film draws in those years. Under it are things that happened then, each with the source it was taken from. Press a picture to play the film from that period.

    What's real here, and what is drawn

    • RealThe ground and the three shores: as first mapped in 1802, as mapped in 1834, and as it is today. They are the same data as in the c.1700 world and today’s shoreline: today’s elevation, the University of Toronto’s Historical Hydrography of Toronto, and OpenStreetMap.
    • RealEvery building that stands at the end of the film: about 9,000 footprints with their heights, from OpenStreetMap. Where a height is not recorded it is estimated from the number of storeys, or set to 6 m.
    • RealToday’s main roads, railways and subway tracks, from OpenStreetMap.
    • RealThe years of fifteen landmarks, which go up in the film in the year they opened or were finished: St Lawrence Hall (1850), St James Cathedral (1853), the Gooderham Building (1892), Old City Hall (1899), Union Station (1927), the Royal York (1929), the Bank of Commerce tower (1931), City Hall (1965), the TD tower (1967), Commerce Court West (1972), First Canadian Place (1975), the CN Tower (1976), Scotia Plaza (1988), SkyDome (1989) and the arena now called Scotiabank Arena (1999). Most are in the timeline above with their sources. The years for St James Cathedral, the Gooderham Building, Commerce Court West, First Canadian Place, Scotia Plaza and the arena are from their Wikipedia articles and have not been checked against another source.
    • RealThe first train (1853), the storm that cut the peninsula into islands (1858), and the subway under Yonge Street (1954): see the timeline. The University line (1963), the Bloor–Danforth line (1966) and the Spadina line (1978) are drawn in the years given by Wikipedia’s articles on the lines, not checked elsewhere.
    • MixedThe number of people under the year. From 1834 it follows the census counts on the notes page, joined by straight lines; the area counted grew over time, so the figure is for a bigger Toronto in later years than the film shows. Before 1834 no number is given.
    • ModelThe year each ordinary building appears. A building’s year is set from how far it is from the first ten blocks of the town (the city’s edge is taken to reach 850 m by 1834, 1.75 km by 1861, 3.6 km by 1901), from its height (the taller, the later; nothing over 90 m before 1967 except the landmarks), and from when the land under it was made. Two buildings side by side may be decades out. The pattern is the point: outward first, then upward.
    • ModelThe small houses of the 1800s. None of them is in the data, so about 24,000 are drawn on a regular grid turned the way Toronto’s streets run, one and a half times life size so they can be seen. Each is pulled down in the year the building that stands there today goes up. Where each stood is not from any map.
    • ModelHow fast the forest was cut, the year each road appears, and the year each piece of new land was filled between the 1834 shore and today’s. The filling is drawn spreading evenly outward from the 1834 shore between 1855 and 1967; the real work went in bursts.
    • DrawnThe look: blue ink, hatching for shade, the red ruled lines, the old paper and its stains. The subway runs underground and is marked in red on the surface, as a plan would mark it. The railways appear all at once in the 1850s though they were built over decades.
    • Not yetWharves and ships, bridges, parks, street names, and anything outside the piece of downtown the building data covers: beyond it the film shows only the small houses and the main roads. The Mississaugas of the Credit, on whose land the town was built, are named in the timeline but not shown. Nothing here has been reviewed by them.

    The shoreline data are adapted from the University of Toronto’s Historical Hydrography of Toronto, CC BY-SA 4.0. Buildings, roads and railways © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.
