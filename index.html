    02 / A city drawn in maple leaves

    Toronto, Redrawn.

    The city’s limits from 1834 to 1998, laid down leaf by leaf, over the shore and creeks of seven old maps.

    · Code & sound · In 3D

    Leaf colour: when its ground joined
    A silent glimpse. Play the film to hear the notes.
    Drag the map to turn it.
    How it was made, and what it leaves out

    The film is drawn live in your browser, not played from a video. Each maple leaf stands for a patch of ground inside a mapped city limit: about hectares in the old city and in the boroughs, leaves in all. A leaf falls when its ground joins the city and keeps the colour of that year. Older ground stands on a higher terrace. In 1998 every leaf turns the same red.

    The blue lines are the shore, marsh and creeks as seven maps drew them between 1802 and 1878, traced from the georeferenced sheets by the University of Toronto’s Map & Data Library. Each map covers a different area, so a creek that vanishes between two maps may only have run off the sheet. Straight sheet edges are not drawn.

    The last scene compares Chewett’s 1834 shore with today’s. The gold leaves are ground inside the 1834 limits that his map shows as water and today’s shoreline shows as land. The film does not date that filling; it only shows both ends.

      What it leaves out: Metropolitan Toronto began in 1954 with thirteen municipalities, but only the six that remained after 1967 are drawn, because boundaries for the other seven were not found. Annexation 48, Victoria Park Avenue, is a strip of road too narrow to show. The areas ( km² by 1967, km² in 1998) are measured from the simplified outlines, which include some water. Terrace heights are a drawing device, not ground levels. Today’s roads, railways and rivers are there for orientation and are not historical.
